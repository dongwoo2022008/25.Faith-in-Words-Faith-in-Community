# Section 2 (revised, with theory) and three manuscript inserts — 2026-09-18

## 2. Two ways religion enters a loan file

Signaling theory has a simple test for whether a message should be believed. A message is credible when sending it falsely is costly, or when the sender's interests happen to line up with the receiver's; a message that anyone can send at no cost, to an audience that would like to hear it, is cheap talk, and a rational receiver discounts it (Spence, 1973; Crawford and Sobel, 1982; Farrell and Rabin, 1996). The sociology of religion arrived at a parallel distinction by a different road. Iannaccone (1992) and later Sosis and Bressler (2003) argued that religious communities survive on costly signals — time, money, dietary and behavioral restrictions — precisely because such costs screen out free riders, and that verbal profession alone screens out no one. Religious commitment that costs something is informative about the person; religious commitment that costs nothing is not. [서지 원문 확인 필요]

A loan application gives a borrower both kinds of channel, and the two show up in LendingClub's data in different fields.

The job title is the costly channel. Reporting oneself as a pastor or chaplain is verifiable, ties the borrower to an institution, and is not something one adds to a loan request to make it more appealing. It also comes bundled with attributes that plausibly lower default regardless of belief — an income that is modest but institutionally paid, a congregation that observes the borrower's conduct, and a professional identity in which unpaid debt carries a reputational cost beyond the credit file. I do not try to separate belief from these correlates. The hypothesis is about the information content of the affiliation as the lender observes it.

**H1.** Conditional on credit score, income, debt burden, loan terms, state and origination year, borrowers whose stated occupation is a religious vocation default less (odds ratio below one).

The description is the cheap channel. "God bless" costs nothing to write, cannot be verified, and is addressed to strangers who are deciding whether to lend. Two mechanisms make its expected sign positive. The first is selection: the borrowers who feel moved to invoke faith in a request for money are disproportionately those whose situation gives them reason to — the same descriptions thank the reader in advance, describe hardship, and ask for help at several times the base rate. The second is substitution: a borrower with strong verifiable facts to offer tends to offer them, so appeals to faith concentrate where such facts are thin. Under either mechanism the test is not whether religious language correlates with default, which Netzer et al. (2019) already showed, but whether it does so *after* the length, tone, hardship and gratitude content it travels with are held fixed. That residual is what the cheap-talk reading predicts should remain, and what a "religious language is just distress language" reading predicts should not.

**H2.** Conditional on the same credit and loan controls and on the length, moral, hardship, gratitude, family, appeal and business content of the description, borrowers who use religious language default more (odds ratio above one).

The theory also makes a prediction inside the description itself that I did not pre-register but can check. If cost is what makes a religious signal informative, then a description that reports religious *practice* — a tithe line in a monthly budget, employment by a church, a mission trip to be paid for — should look more like the occupational signal than like "God bless," because the practice costs something and the phrase does not. Section 5.2 reports that it does.

H1 and H2 are the only confirmatory tests. I test each one-sided with a Holm correction and stated in advance what a failure would mean: without H2 the paper would be a study of embodied faith with expressed faith as a null result; without H1 surviving stable-occupation controls, the clergy effect would have to be read as secure employment rather than religious affiliation. As it happened, H1 survived everything and H2 survived its pre-specified test but not a stricter one I had also set out in advance (Section 5.2); I report both outcomes as the rules required.

## Insert A — Section 3.2, after the dictionary paragraph (measurement completeness)

One might worry that a dictionary misses religious content expressed without its keywords — "the man upstairs," "a higher power," "keep us in your prayers." I checked. I trained a classifier on the 583 reader-confirmed religious descriptions against 4,000 random unflagged ones, with every explicit religious word masked so that the model could only learn from surrounding context, and scored the 122,611 unflagged descriptions. A reader then coded stratified samples from the top of the ranking: none of 59 in the top 500 and none of 58 in the next 1,500 contained religious content, and 3 of 240 further down did. A supplementary list of implicit phrases found 11 more (mostly "godsend" and "karma"). Religious content that the dictionary misses exists, but on the order of a few dozen descriptions in the whole file. The 681 flagged loans are, for practical purposes, the population; the imprecision of the narrative estimate in Section 5.2 is a matter of how few borrowers write this way, not of how I found them.

## Insert B — Section 5.2, replacing the sentence on subtypes

Broken down by the reader's coding, formulaic invocations — "God bless," "prayers answered" — carry the effect (odds ratio 1.39, p = 0.001, 467 loans); identity claims point the same way on too few loans to tell (1.46, n = 34); and descriptions that report religious practice — a tithe in the budget, church employment, a mission trip — show no elevated default at all (0.99, n = 82; raw default rate 14.6 percent, below the sample average). The cell is small, but it is the cell the argument of Section 2 singles out: where the narrative describes faith as something done rather than said, it behaves like the occupational signal.

## Insert C — Section 6 (Discussion), one paragraph on economic magnitude

Neither signal is priced. LendingClub set interest rates by sub-grade and date, so two loans in the same sub-grade in the same month carried the same rate whatever the job title or the description said. Against loans in the same sub-grade-year cell, clergy loans defaulted at 13.3 rather than 17.4 percent and lost 5.5 rather than 7.7 cents per dollar funded, a gap of 2.2 cents over an average 41-month life, or roughly 64 basis points a year of yield an investor holding clergy loans earned above the cell. Loans with religious language went the other way: 20.0 against 15.9 percent default, 7.1 against 6.1 cents lost, about 28 basis points a year below the cell. These are not large numbers, and I do not claim an investor could have traded on them — the signals flag a fraction of one percent of loans. They are the size of the information the market left on the table, stated in the units the market uses.
