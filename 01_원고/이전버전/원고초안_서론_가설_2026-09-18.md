# Faith in Words and Faith at Work: Two Religious Signals with Opposite Credit-Risk Content in Online Lending

*Draft of Section 1 (Introduction) and Section 2 (Hypotheses) — 2026-09-18. Numbers cited are from the preliminary analyses in the project files and will be replaced by the final tables.*

## 1. Introduction

Borrowers on early peer-to-peer platforms were asked to say something about themselves. LendingClub gave them a free-text box; some wrote nothing, some wrote a paragraph about credit cards, and a small number wrote about God. "Thank you and God bless." "As a Christian I believe in paying my debts." "My monthly budget: tithe $529, rent $900…" These sentences are rare — I count 717 of them among 125,811 loan descriptions filed between 2008 and 2014 — and it is tempting to treat them as noise. Netzer, Lemaire and Herzenstein (2019) did not. In their study of Prosper applications, mentions of God were among the words that appeared more often in the text of borrowers who later defaulted. They reported it as one lexical signal among many, and moved on.

I want to stay with it, because the same platform contains a second, quieter trace of religion that points the other way. LendingClub also asked borrowers for their job title. About one in four hundred wrote "pastor," "minister," "chaplain," "rabbi," or "missionary." Those borrowers default less — 13.3 percent against 20.0 percent in the raw data, and roughly 30 percent less after I condition on grade, FICO, income, debt-to-income, loan terms, state, and year. The two facts sit uneasily together. Borrowers who talk about faith in their loan request are, if anything, worse credit risks than their scores suggest; borrowers whose working lives are organized around faith are better ones. Same platform, same years, same controls, opposite signs.

This paper is about that contrast. My claim is narrow. I do not argue that religion causes repayment, and I do not offer a religious credit score; the signals are too rare to move a predictive model, and I show that they do not. What I argue is that religion reaches a credit market through two channels that carry different information, and that the difference is legible in ordinary loan data once one looks for it. Faith that is *expressed* — invoked in a request for money — behaves like cheap talk in the signaling sense: it costs nothing to write, it clusters with hardship, gratitude and appeals for help, and its association with default survives every text control I can construct but is not strong. Faith that is *embodied* — a vocation with a congregation, a salary, and a reputation attached — behaves like a costly commitment. It is not something one adds to a loan application for effect, and its association with default is large, stable across a decade, and reproduces on a second platform.

The finding matters for three literatures that have mostly not spoken to each other. The first is the work on borrower text in credit markets (Netzer et al., 2019; Nowak, Ross and Yencha, 2018; Sanz-Guerrero and Arroyo, 2024). That literature has established that words predict default, and has been less interested in *which* words carry information of their own rather than proxying for circumstance. The second is the literature on religiosity and financial behavior, which works almost entirely at the level of counties or countries — religious adherence rates and mortgage delinquency (Li and Ucar, 2022), county religiosity and mortgage misrepresentation (Conklin, Diop and Qiu, 2022), Islamic loans in Pakistan (Baele, Farooq and Ongena, 2014). Individual-level evidence is scarce because individual religion is rarely observed in loan files. Here it is, twice, and the effects I find do not depend on where the borrower lives: they are the same inside and outside the Bible Belt. The third is the older signaling tradition (Spence, 1973; Farrell and Rabin, 1996) that distinguishes messages that cost the sender something from messages that do not. Religious identity turns out to be a clean case, because the same underlying trait shows up in the data once as a message and once as a commitment.

I should be direct about what the design can and cannot do. There is no instrument and no natural experiment. Clergy differ from other borrowers in ways I cannot fully observe, and the most obvious alternative explanation — that a pastor's salary is simply steadier than a contractor's — is one I take seriously rather than dismiss. I address it by controlling for eight other occupation groups with stable public or institutional employment (teachers, police, firefighters, military, government, nurses, postal and transit workers), by comparing clergy to those groups directly, and by asking how strong an unobserved confounder would have to be, in the sense of Oster (2019) and Cinelli and Hazlett (2020), to erase the estimate. The answer is: about twelve times as strong as employment tenure. That is not proof of a religious mechanism. It is a statement about how much a skeptic has to assume.

The narrative result deserves a more guarded reading, and I give it one. The estimate of roughly 3 percentage points of additional default risk for religious language is stable across specifications — length matching, a battery of moral, hardship and gratitude dictionaries, a placebo test against a thousand random word sets of equal frequency — and it passes a pre-specified test with Holm correction. But it is estimated from 681 loans, and a double machine learning estimate that conditions on a low-dimensional representation of the full text leaves it just outside conventional significance. I report all of this. The paper's contribution does not rest on the narrative estimate being large; it rests on the two signals having opposite signs under identical conditioning, which every specification reproduces.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 sets out the two hypotheses and the reasoning behind them. Section 3 describes the LendingClub data, the construction of both religious indicators, and the Prosper replication sample. Section 4 presents the main estimates, Section 5 the robustness and sensitivity analyses, and Section 6 discusses what the contrast does and does not imply, including a brief look at behavior after default. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Hypotheses

The signaling literature gives a simple way to think about what a borrower reveals. A message is informative in equilibrium when it is costly to send falsely, or when the sender's interests are aligned with the receiver's; otherwise it is cheap talk and rational lenders should discount it (Spence, 1973; Crawford and Sobel, 1982; Farrell and Rabin, 1996). Religious content can enter a loan file in either form.

Consider first the job title. Declaring oneself a pastor is verifiable, tied to an institution, and not something a borrower chooses in order to obtain a loan. The occupation also comes bundled with features that plausibly lower default apart from any belief: a visible community that observes financial conduct, an income that is modest but institutionally paid, and a professional identity in which unpaid debt carries a reputational cost beyond the credit file. I do not attempt to separate belief from these correlates; the hypothesis is about the information content of the affiliation as observed.

**H1.** Conditional on credit score, income, debt burden, loan terms, state and origination year, borrowers whose stated occupation is a religious vocation have a lower probability of default (odds ratio below one).

Consider next the description. Writing "God bless" or "I have faith that this loan will be funded" is free. It cannot be verified, it is written to an audience of lenders, and it is disproportionately written by borrowers who are also describing hardship, thanking the reader in advance, and asking for help. Two mechanisms push in the same direction. One is selection: borrowers who feel the need to invoke faith in a financial request may be those whose situation gives them reason to. The other is substitution: a borrower with strong verifiable facts to offer tends to offer them, so appeals to faith concentrate where such facts are thin. Under either mechanism the expected sign is positive, and under either it is the *residual* association — after length, tone and hardship language are held fixed — that matters, because the question is whether the religious content itself carries information beyond the circumstances it accompanies.

**H2.** Conditional on the same credit and loan controls and on the length, moral, hardship, gratitude, family, appeal and business content of the description, borrowers who use religious language have a higher probability of default (odds ratio above one).

H1 and H2 are the only confirmatory tests in the paper. I test each one-sided with a Holm correction across the pair. Everything else — the placebo occupations, the fake-dictionary permutation, the entropy-balanced and double-machine-learning estimates, the Prosper replication, the hazard timing, and the coded subtypes of religious language — is reported as robustness or exploration and is not used to adjudicate the hypotheses. I also state in advance what I would conclude if one of them failed. If H2 did not survive the text controls, the paper would become a study of embodied faith alone, with expressed faith as a null result worth reporting. If H1 did not survive the stable-occupation controls, I would have to read the clergy effect as a feature of secure institutional employment rather than of religious affiliation. Neither turned out to be the case, but the reader should know the rules were set before the results were in.
