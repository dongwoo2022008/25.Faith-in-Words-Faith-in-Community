# Writing benchmark brief (2026-09-19)

Benchmarks (user-supplied PDFs): Hertzberg, Liberman & Paravisini (2018, RFS 31(9)); Netzer, Lemaire & Herzenstein (2019, JMR 56(6)); Iyer, Khwaja, Luttmer & Shue (2016, Management Science 62(6)). Observations are structural only; no sentences or phrases are recorded.

## Measured
- Sentence length (words): HLP mean 29.8 / median 26 / SD 21.6; Netzer 25.4 / 23 / 17.0; Iyer 27.3 / 22 / 18.1. Long and short sentences alternate; SD is large in all three.
- Hedging vocabulary: "consistent with" is the dominant interpretive phrase (HLP 14, Netzer 9, Iyer 7). "we find" is the dominant result verb (10 / 16 / 28). "associated with" carries the descriptive results in the non-causal paper (Netzer 34). "causal" appears only where the design supports it (HLP, Iyer) and in Netzer not at all.
- Decorative openers are rare: "Interestingly" 2 (Netzer), "Importantly" 1 each (HLP, Iyer), "It is important to note" 3 (HLP). None of the three uses "Surprisingly", "Notably", "Clearly".
- "the first" appears 7–13 times per paper, but as "first evidence"/"first direct evidence" only where the design is a natural experiment (HLP); Netzer and Iyer use "first" mostly as an ordinal.

## Introduction architecture
- HLP (RFS): 1 paragraph theory premise → 1 paragraph "evidence is circumstantial because …" (the gap stated as a measurement problem) → 1 paragraph "in this paper we …" with the identification idea in plain words → 1 paragraph institutional setting → 1 paragraph empirical strategy with the identification assumption spelled out → 2 paragraphs of results, each with a number and its interpretation → 2 paragraphs on mechanism, structured as "two explanations, different predictions, which one the data fit" → 1 paragraph alternative behavioral explanation and why it is unlikely → 1 paragraph relation to theory literature → contribution. About 12 paragraphs; results appear by paragraph 6.
- Netzer (JMR): opens with a two-borrower vignette, then states the research question, then the claim. Literature is organized as a taxonomy of information categories (four kinds), not as a list of papers. Method and headline number appear in the same paragraph. A separate paragraph interprets the word-level findings and explicitly declines a stronger claim ("we do not claim…"). One contribution paragraph, positioned against "small-scale" prior work. Roadmap last.
- Iyer (MS): 2 paragraphs on the screening problem and why hard information is the traditional answer → 1 paragraph on what peer markets add and their downside → "In this paper we examine…" → setting → the identifying feature of the data (researchers see what lenders do not) → results in sequence, each with its benchmark and number → heterogeneity → caveats stated inside the introduction, not deferred.
- Common: the research question is stated in one sentence within the first three paragraphs; the gap is stated as "what prior evidence cannot distinguish", not as "no one has studied"; every result sentence in the introduction carries a magnitude; contributions are two or three and are stated against a specific literature; caveats appear early (HLP paragraph 8, Iyer paragraph 9), not only in a limitations section.

## Results exposition
- Table sentences name the table, the column, and what the column does before the number ("Column 1 of Table 2 presents…", "Table 2 first examines…", "Rows (1)–(7) of Table 4 show…"). Numbers follow in the same or next sentence, then a benchmark for magnitude (base rate, average, comparison group), then one interpretive sentence.
- HLP states the economic magnitude by dividing the estimate by the base rate and says which is the "baseline" result and why. Iyer benchmarks every accuracy number against two comparators. Netzer converts model gains into ROI.
- Null results are reported with the estimate and the statement that it is not distinguishable from zero, and then interpreted (HLP: "not a surprising result").
- Robustness is organized as objections: each paragraph names the concern, the test, and the outcome.

## Mechanism and strength of claims
- HLP: two candidate explanations → their different predictions → which the data match; "inconsistent with"/"consistent with" throughout; suggestive evidence labeled as such in the same sentence.
- Netzer: interpretation of word patterns is explicitly non-causal and non-accusatory (declines to say borrowers were deceptive).
- Iyer: each analysis section carries its own caveat sentence; "only suggestive" is stated before the analysis, not after.

## Limitations
- Placed inside the relevant results or mechanism section (all three), and again briefly in the introduction (HLP, Iyer). No stand-alone list of limitations; the conclusion restates what was established.

## Voice and mechanics
- First person plural throughout ("we find", "we examine", "we caveat"). Present tense for results and claims; past tense for data construction events.
- Paragraphs are long (typically 6–12 sentences) and each opens with the claim or the object of the paragraph.
- Footnotes carry the parenthetical literature and the secondary tests, keeping the main text linear.

## Implications for the manuscript (revision mode)
1. Introduction: keep the vignette opening (matches Netzer), state the research question in one sentence by paragraph 3, make every result sentence carry a magnitude, move the caveat paragraph earlier (already present), and cut the contribution list to the three that answer "what do we now know".
2. Results: precede every number with the table/column reference and follow it with a base-rate benchmark; report nulls with estimate + "indistinguishable from zero" + interpretation.
3. Mechanism: frame as competing explanations with differing predictions (already partly done in 2.1); label suggestive evidence in the same sentence.
4. Voice: single author → "I" (benchmarks use "we" because they are co-authored; keep "I" consistently).
5. Trim decorative openers and any "increases/reduces" applied to own estimates.
