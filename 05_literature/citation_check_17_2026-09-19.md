# 서술형 인용 원문 대조 결과 (v9, 2026-09-19)

원고 v9에서 괄호 인용을 서술형으로 바꾼 17곳을 각 논문 PDF 원문과 대조한 결과와 반영 내역.

| # | 문헌 | 판정 | 문제 | 원문 근거 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Deller, Conroy and Markeson (2018) | PARTLY | The paper does not measure social capital as an outcome. It treats congregations as a proxy or source of social capital and regresses a county proprietorship index on them. 'Predicts local social capital' is therefore not something the paper tests. The business-activity part is supported (U.S. counties, cross-sectional association). | We find that communities with a large concentration of religious congregations have a correspondingly higher level of small business activity. (p. 365, abstract) |
| 2 | Deng, Liu and Yan (2025) | PARTLY | Direction is right and the paper does measure social capital (trust and trustworthiness) in supplementary analyses. But the main outcome is corporate tax avoidance, the treatment is specifically Protestant missionary activity c. 1920 in China (instrumented by historical disaster frequency), and 'historical religious activity' generalizes beyond this. Social capital is a secondary, channel-level result. 'Show' is acceptable for an IV design, but 'find' is safer since the social-capital evidence is correlational survey evidence. | Protestant activities in 1920 significantly reduced ... corporate tax avoidance ... Further analyses of a survey-based dataset and a proprietary peer-to-peer lending dataset show that Protestantism fostered social capital that persists in the long run. (p. 1) |
| 3 | Baele, Farooq and Ongena (2014) | SUPPORTED | Supported, including the within-borrower comparison (Pakistan, 2006-2008). 'Comparable borrowers' is if anything an understatement, since they compare the same borrower. | the default rate of Islamic loans is less than half the default rate of conventional loans. (p. 141); with borrower fixed effects, 'the same borrower is less likely to default on Islamic than on conventional' loans. |
| 4 | Iyer, Khwaja, Luttmer and Shue (2016) | SUPPORTED | Supported for Prosper. Nuance: lenders see only a credit category, and much of their extra predictive power comes from standard financial information, with soft/nonstandard information relatively more important for lower-quality borrowers. 'What lies outside the credit score' covers both, so the wording holds. | peer lenders predict an individual's likelihood of defaulting on a loan with 45% greater accuracy than the borrower's exact credit score (unobserved by the lenders, who only see a credit category). (p. 1554, abstract) |
| 5 | Ghatak and Guinnane (1999) | PARTLY | The paper's framework has four problems, not three: adverse selection, moral hazard, auditing (costly state verification) and enforcement. The three-part paraphrase drops auditing (or silently folds it into 'watched'). Also, this is an analytical framework rather than a catalogue. | (a) ... what kind of a risk the potential borrower is (adverse selection), (b) ... utilize the loan properly (moral hazard), (c) ... how her project really did (auditing costs) and (d) ... force the borrower to repay (enforcement). (pp. 196-197) |
| 6 | Karlan (2007) | PARTLY | Direction and mechanism are right, but 'rather than through selection alone' implies that selection also contributes. The design rules selection out, and the paper says monitoring and enforcement, 'and not selection', explain the effect (p. F53). 'Socially connected' means geographic proximity or cultural similarity (FINCA-Peru). | I exploit a quasi-random group formation process to find evidence of peers successfully monitoring and enforcing joint-liability loans. Individuals with stronger social connections ... (i.e., either living closer or being of a similar culture) have higher repayment (p. F52) |
| 7 | Feigenberg, Field and Pande (2013) | SUPPORTED | Supported: groups were randomly assigned to meet weekly rather than monthly in the first loan cycle, and default on the second loan fell to about a third (cut by two thirds). 'Show' is appropriate for a randomized design. The absolute effect is small (5.2 pp). | Those who met weekly during their first loan cycle were three times (5.2 percentage points) less likely to default on their subsequent loan, despite the fact that all clients had reverted to the same repayment schedule. (p. 1461) |
| 8 | Giné and Karlan (2014) | SUPPORTED | Supported (Philippines, Green Bank, randomized conversion to individual liability). The manuscript's gloss that 'the group disciplines' fits the authors' own point that reputation and social influence remain after conversion. | In both, groups still held weekly meetings. We find no increase in short-run or long-run default (p. 65, abstract); the change 'did not remove all social influences on repayment' (Section 3). |
| 9 | Al-Azzam, Hill and Sarangi (2012) | PARTLY | Wrong level of analysis and outcome. Religiosity is measured as the share of the group's members who pray five times a day, and the dependent variable is days of late repayment for the group (160 groups, Microfund for Women, female borrowers), not the individual borrower's own repayment or default. The authors also raise a possible confound from husbands' or parents' control. | we measure the intensity of religious practice across groups by considering the percentage of group members who pray five times a day ... More religious groups tend to have fewer days of late repayment. (pp. 407-409) |
| 10 | Kirchmaier, Prüfer and Trautmann (2018) | PARTLY | Substance is right, but the comparison is religious versus non-religious people in a representative Dutch panel, not 'than average' or 'everyone else'. Also Catholics betray less than the non-religious (p. 284). In the narrative form 'their' now comes right after the author names and reads as the authors' attitudes; the antecedent ('religious people') is in the previous sentence. | religious people are less accepting of unethical economic behavior (e.g., tax evasion, bribery) ... They are equally likely as non-religious people to betray trust in an experimental game, where social behavior is unobservable (p. 282, abstract) |
| 11 | Agarwal, Chomsisengphet and Zhang (2017); Davaadorj et al. (2024) | SUPPORTED | Both parts are supported. Agarwal's result is not conditional on the crisis and is not due to strategic default, but the sample is borrowers of a U.S. subprime lender. The authors discuss several explanations that are not mutually exclusive, so the manuscript's next sentence ('the natural reading is expertise') is the manuscript's own reading. 'Skilled' in Davaadorj means O*NET Job Zone 5 titles, and 'priced accordingly' corresponds to lower interest rates. Adding 'subprime' would be more precise but is optional. | Agarwal: 'financial professionals are 16% less likely to become delinquent than non-financial professionals.' (p. 1). Davaadorj: 'skilled borrowers ... receive lower loan interest rate and default less frequently than do their non-skilled peers.' (abstract) |
| 12 | Noussair, Trautmann, van de Kuilen and Vellekoop (2013) | SUPPORTED | Content is supported (representative Dutch LISS panel with an incentivized risk measure). The authors hedge ('suggest'), and the mechanism evidence is correlational: belief measures are unrelated to risk aversion, while attendance and prayer are related. 'Find ... runs through' is slightly stronger than the paper, so a hedged verb fits better. | our data suggest that the link between risk aversion and religion is driven by social aspects of church membership, rather than by religious beliefs themselves. (p. 165, abstract) |
| 13 | Hasan, Kiesel and Noth (2025) | SUPPORTED | Direction is correct (less over-indebtedness where Catholics outnumber Protestants). German county-level data (2011 Microcensus), with IV robustness. | We find that over-indebtedness is lower in regions in which Catholics outweigh Protestants, indicating that Catholics' forgiveness culture and stricter enforcement of rules by Protestants serve as explanations (p. 1013, abstract) |
| 14 | Cassar, Crowley and Wydick (2007) | PARTLY | The 'loan repayment' is contribution or repayment in an experimental microfinance game, not repayment of actual loans. Written as is, the claim reads as field repayment data. Also, generalized trust is found to be less important, not strictly null. | evidence from field experiments in South Africa and Armenia, in which subjects participate in trust and microfinance games ... personal trust between group members and social homogeneity are more important to group loan repayment than general societal trust (p. F85) |
| 15 | Traunmüller (2011) | SUPPORTED | Supported for regional devoutness (church-attendance rates). Caveat for the framing ('a null regional one'): the same paper finds positive contextual effects of a Protestant regional context and lower trust in Catholic-dominated regions, so regional religious composition is not null. 'Has no contextual effect' is slightly stronger than 'could not be detected'. | while church attendance is a powerful predictor for social trust, a context effect for regional levels of devoutness could not be detected. (p. 346, abstract) |
| 16 | Hertzberg, Liberman and Paravisini (2018) | NOT SUPPORTED | The paper finds the opposite of 'goes unpriced'. Borrowers with higher unobserved risk self-select into the higher-rate 60-month loan, so the maturity menu prices private information, and LendingClub later cut the 36-month APR by about the size of the fall in default. The only 'unpriced' element is transitional: within the experiment window the short-term rate was held fixed. 'Show' is fine for their natural experiment, but the content is misstated. | screening on maturity allows the lender to charge prices that are commensurate with borrowers' unobserved default risk. (p. 3534); after the sample period 'LC adjusted the APR of the 36-month loan' down by roughly 0.8% (pp. 3564-3565) |
| 17 | Anglin, Milanov and Short (2023) | NOT SUPPORTED | Direction is reversed. On Kiva, religious expression slows or reduces funding (role incongruity with the entrepreneur role), more so for women. The penalty is only attenuated, not reversed, among more religious lenders in the experiment. The manuscript's inference ('consistent with the audience being one that would like to hear it') is contradicted by this paper. | Our field study incorporating 253,130 loans from Kiva reveals that religious expression negatively influences funding, particularly for women. ... the negative influence of religious expression is attenuated when individual lenders exhibit higher levels of religiosity. (p. 397, abstract) |
