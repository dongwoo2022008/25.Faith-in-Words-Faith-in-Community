Dear Editors,

I am submitting the manuscript "Faith in Words, Faith in Community: Religious Affiliation, Religious Language, and Default in Online Consumer Credit" for consideration as a research article in the *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.

The paper asks what a credit market can learn from religion, and its answer is about organization rather than belief. Religion enters a consumer-credit file in two ways: as an affiliation a borrower reports in a job title, and as language a borrower chooses to write in a loan request. In 1.34 million terminated LendingClub loans, the two carry opposite information. Borrowers employed by religious institutions, church secretaries and administrators as well as pastors, default about five percentage points less than observably identical borrowers. The advantage is larger where congregations are denser, holds within three-digit ZIP areas, does not respond to local unemployment shocks, and replicates on Prosper. Borrowers who invoke faith in their description default about three percentage points more, an estimate that is stable in sign but loses precision once the full text is conditioned on, and I report it that way. Neither signal is priced.

I believe the paper fits the journal for three reasons. First, its central claim concerns an institution: a religious congregation appears to work for its members the way the group works in a group-lending scheme, enforcing repayment through ties the lender never sees. This extends the social-collateral logic from groups a lender organizes to a community a borrower belongs to. Second, the second half of the paper is about communication under asymmetric information. A short framework shows why a costless, unverifiable message can carry information opposite to its sender's intent, and why the two religious signals can take opposite signs without any difference in belief. Third, the paper connects work that this journal has published on religion and economic behavior (for example, Valencia Caicedo, Dohmen and Pondorfer, 2023; Bentzen, 2021) with its work on default in the same LendingClub market (Croux, Jagtiani, Korivi and Vulanovic, 2020), which documents that occupation predicts default but does not ask what a religious occupation reveals.

The design is observational, and the manuscript says so plainly. The two hypotheses and the rules for downgrading them were fixed before estimation, and I report where the second one failed the stricter of those rules. The analysis uses the public LendingClub and Prosper loan files and public county data from the U.S. Religion Census and the Bureau of Labor Statistics.
