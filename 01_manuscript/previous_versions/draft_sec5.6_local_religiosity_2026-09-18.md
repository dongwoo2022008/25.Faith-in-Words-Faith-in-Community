## 5.6 Individual religion, local religion (insert for Section 5; replaces the Bible Belt sentence in 5.1)

The religiosity literature in finance is almost entirely about places. County adherence rates predict lower mortgage delinquency (Li and Ucar, 2022), less mortgage misrepresentation (Conklin, Diop and Qiu, 2022), and lower microfinance losses across countries (Gyapong, Gyimah and Ahmed, 2021). My signals are about persons, and it is worth asking whether they are the same thing seen from closer up — whether a pastor defaults less because he is a pastor, or because pastors live in religious counties and religious counties default less.

I merged the 2010 U.S. Religion Census county file (Grammich et al., 2012) [서지 확인] to LendingClub's three-digit ZIP codes through the Census ZCTA–county relationship file, weighting counties by population within each ZIP3 area, and built two measures: total adherents per thousand residents and congregations per ten thousand. The merge covers 99.97 percent of the sample across 894 ZIP3 areas.

Three things emerge. First, neither signal is a local effect in disguise. With both regional measures added to the full specification, the clergy odds ratio is 0.686 and the religious-language odds ratio 1.280, unchanged to the second decimal from Tables 2 and 3. Second, local religiosity does not do in this market what it does in mortgage markets. Within state, borrowers from more religious ZIP3 areas default slightly *more* (odds ratio 1.04 per standard deviation of adherence, 1.06 per standard deviation of congregation density), and the evangelical share is unrelated to default. I do not have an explanation and offer none; unsecured consumer credit differs from mortgage credit in ways that may matter, and the within-state residual variation in religiosity is correlated with income and urbanicity in ways that state fixed effects and my income control may not fully absorb. I flag it as a contrast with the county literature rather than a contradiction of it.

Third, and this is the result I find most informative, the clergy effect is not uniform across places: it is larger where congregations are denser. The interaction of clergy status with congregation density has an odds ratio of 0.84 per standard deviation (p = 0.003); by tercile of local adherence the clergy odds ratio runs 0.71 in the least religious third of areas, 0.83 in the middle, and 0.58 in the most religious. If the occupational signal reflected only a pastor's private commitment, there is no reason it should depend on how many other churches stand nearby. If part of it reflects the reputational cost of default inside a community that observes financial conduct, it should — and it does. Religious language, by contrast, shows no interaction with local religiosity at all (1.07, p = 0.50), which is what one expects of a message whose meaning does not depend on who is listening. I present this as a pattern consistent with a community-monitoring mechanism, not as a test that isolates it.
